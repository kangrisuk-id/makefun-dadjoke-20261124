# Dad Joke Showdown — candidates and tournament

The judge (a father of three, briefed in the kit's RULES.txt) weighs pairs in
this order:

1. A dad joke a father would tell at the table, within 200 characters.
2. Lands its pun cleanly — the turn understood on the first read.
3. Feels fresh rather than told a thousand times, inside the dad tradition.
4. Tight — every word earns its place, punchline at the end.
5. Reads naturally aloud, dinner-table rhythm.

Gate: `entry.txt` must be UTF-8, 1–200 characters, not only white space.

## Round of 40 → 16 (judged on criteria 1–3 first)

- 1 beats 40 — 40 is a one-liner quip, not a table joke with a turn (crit. 2).
- 34 beats 13 — 13 is the single most told dad joke alive (crit. 3), 34's turn is just as clean (crit. 2 tie).
- 35 beats 27 — both clean turns; 35's setup is a dad doing the most dad thing there is (crit. 1, 3).
- 28 beats 6 — 6 is a greeting-card regular (crit. 3); 28's "cold shoulder" lands first-read (crit. 2).
- 29 beats 23 — 23 is a schoolyard antique (crit. 3); 29's "recalculating" is a fresh turn in full tradition (crit. 3).
- 30 beats 20 — 20 has no real turn; 30 literalises an idiom, understood instantly (crit. 2).
- 3 beats 17 — both classics; 3's deadpan reversal is the stronger table delivery (crit. 5).
- 10 beats 2 — 2 is told a thousand times (crit. 3); 10's "big step forward" is nearly as worn but less.
- 31 beats 5 — 5 is a spelling gag that dies out loud (crit. 5); 31's "over their heads" works spoken.
- 9 beats 21 — same shape; 9's hole-in-one turn is cleaner (crit. 2).
- 7 beats 26 — 7's double meaning lands in one beat; 26 needs the pause explained by spelling (crit. 2, 5).
- 33 beats 15 — 15 is a meme caption now (crit. 3); 33's peak/peek survives out loud (crit. 5).
- 39 beats 12 — 12 is the most groaned-at chestnut in the book (crit. 3); 39 plays a whole scene in two lines.
- 32 beats 25 — 32 is a proper shaggy-dad construction; 25 is a riddle with a flat answer (crit. 2).
- 18 beats 8 — 18's "put my foot down" is a genuine double turn; 8 is a single flat pun (crit. 2).
- 36 beats 24 — 24 is worn smooth (crit. 3); 36 is fresh, though it leans on voice over pun (noted for later rounds).

Survivors: 1, 3, 7, 9, 10, 18, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32, 33, 34, 35, 36, 39.

## Round of 16 → 8

- 34 beats 1 — 1 is everywhere (crit. 3); 34 is shorter and its punch is the last word (crit. 4).
- 35 beats 39 — 39 is a known librarian chestnut (crit. 3); 35 is two puns deep and still first-read (crit. 2, 3).
- 28 beats 31 — 31's cloud gag has done the rounds (crit. 3); 28 is newer and the fridge's "cold shoulder" is a person-turn, very tellable (crit. 1).
- 29 beats 33 — 33's peak/peek wobbles out loud for some listeners (crit. 5); 29's recalculation is unmistakable (crit. 2).
- 30 beats 36 — 36 has no pun turn, just swagger (crit. 2); 30 turns an idiom into a plot.
- 3 beats 10 — both seasoned; 3's reversal is a story in one breath, 10 is a gym gag (crit. 1).
- 9 beats 7 — 7 is tight but abstract; 9 is a father at a golf course, pure table material (crit. 1).
- 32 beats 18 — 18 is well-worn; 32 builds a little world and pays it off (crit. 3, 4).

Survivors: 3, 9, 28, 29, 30, 32, 34, 35.

## Quarter-finals

- 34 beats 3 — 3 is delightful but long and much-retold (crit. 3, 4); 34 is 11 words, one image, punch last (crit. 4).
- 35 beats 32 — 32 needs its whole setup paragraph to breathe (crit. 4); 35 does the same work in one line.
- 29 beats 9 — 9 is a classic of the clubhouse circuit (crit. 3); 29 is this decade's version of the same dad energy.
- 28 beats 30 — close: both modern, both clean. 30's payoff ("told my boss I loved him") is a situation, not a turn of phrase; 28's payoff IS the phrase — "cold shoulder" from a fridge (crit. 2).

Semi-finalists: 28, 29, 34, 35.

## Semi-finals

- 34 beats 29 — 29 is charming but 19 words of relationship talk before the turn; 34's setup is the joke's own picture and the answer detonates on the last syllable (crit. 4). On crit. 3 both are modern-enough in circulation; 29's "recalculating" has been a greeting-card line for a decade.
- 35 beats 28 — 28 is a fine joke but the fridge has to be "smart" for it to be new, and the personification takes a beat to set up (crit. 2, 4). 35 needs nothing explained: a dad, jokes, ice.

## Final: 34 vs 35

Both are one-line question-and-answer, both end on the pun, both pure table
material (crit. 1, 4, 5 effectively tied).

- Crit. 2 — the turn on first read: 35 asks the listener to hold two senses of
  "icebreaker" (the social one and the literal one) at once; delightful, but
  it is a half-beat of assembly. 34 maps one-to-one: dad = pop, through the
  ice = frozen = the "-sicle". Nothing to assemble; the groan is instant.
  First clear difference goes to 34.
- Crit. 3 — freshness: 35 is the less circulated of the two, a real edge —
  but a modest one, not the "differs by much more" the brief needs to
  overturn an earlier criterion.

**Winner: #34 — "What do you call a dad who falls through the ice? A popsicle."**

Runner-up: #35 — "What do you call a dad telling jokes on a frozen lake? An icebreaker."
